Generative AI app development: costs, models and pitfalls

By Awais Chaudhry, 25 November 2026

Generative AI apps write text, create images, summarise documents, draft emails, describe photos or turn speech into notes. Getting a demo working is quick: a few lines of code can send a prompt to a model and show the answer. Turning that demo into a product people pay for is a different job. This guide covers what a generative AI app costs to build and run, how to choose a model, and the pitfalls we see most often, using our own rate of $50 per hour. We build these apps, including the AI image generation app in our portfolio, so the advice comes from real projects rather than vendor brochures.

What counts as a generative AI app?

Any product where the main output is created by a model rather than written by a developer in advance. Common examples:

  • Text tools: writing assistants, product description generators, report and proposal drafting, email replies.
  • Document tools: summarising contracts, extracting fields from invoices, answering questions about your own files.
  • Image and media tools: image generation from prompts, background removal, product photo variations, avatars.
  • Voice tools: transcription, meeting notes, voice assistants and spoken replies.
  • Agents: assistants that take steps in other systems, such as updating a CRM or booking an appointment.

The model is usually rented through an API from a provider such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or a host for open-source models. Your app is everything around it: the screens, accounts, payments, prompts, data, limits and checks that make the output useful and safe.

Where the hours go

  • Product basics (25 to 40 percent): sign-up, payments, settings, history, admin. These are the same as in any app.
  • Prompt and pipeline design (15 to 25 percent): the instructions, examples, output formats and the steps between them. Good pipelines often split one big prompt into smaller, checkable steps.
  • Data and retrieval (0 to 30 percent): only if the app must use your documents or database. See how RAG works for what this involves.
  • Evaluation and safety (10 to 20 percent): test sets, content filters, refusal rules and human review.
  • Cost and reliability controls (5 to 10 percent): caching, rate limits, retries, fallbacks when a provider is slow or down.

Running costs: the part founders forget

A normal app costs about the same whether a user clicks once or a hundred times. A generative AI app has a cost every time it generates something. Plan for:

  • Model usage. Text models are charged by tokens, roughly pieces of words, both for what you send and what comes back. Image, audio and video models are charged per image, per minute or per second. Long prompts sent with every request are the most common cause of high bills.
  • Hosting and storage. Servers or serverless functions, a database, file storage for generated images, and a vector database if you search documents.
  • Moderation and monitoring. Some content checks are free, others are paid per request. Logging and error tracking have small monthly fees.
  • Maintenance. Providers retire older model versions and change prices. Budget time each quarter to test and move to newer models. Our app maintenance plans include this.

Before we build, we estimate the cost of one typical generation and multiply it by expected usage. If you charge a subscription, the price has to cover your heaviest users, not your average ones, so we add per-user limits from day one.

How to choose a model

There is no single best model. The right one depends on the task, and many good apps use more than one.

Hosted frontier models

The large models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google give the best quality for complex writing, reasoning and long documents, with no servers to manage. They cost more per request and your data passes through the provider, so read their data-use and retention terms. Business API terms are usually stricter than consumer apps about training on your data, but check the current terms for your plan.

Smaller and faster models

Every major provider also offers smaller, cheaper and faster models. They are often good enough for classification, short replies, extraction and routing. A common pattern is to send simple requests to a small model and only hard ones to a large model, which can cut running cost a lot without users noticing.

Open-source models

Open-weight models such as Llama, Mistral, Qwen or Stable Diffusion can run on your own servers or through a hosting service. They make sense when data must stay inside your infrastructure, when volume is high enough that GPU servers cost less than API fees, or when you need to fine-tune heavily. The trade-off is setup, GPU cost and ongoing operations work. Check each model's licence too: some restrict commercial use or very large companies.

A practical selection process

  1. Write 30 to 100 real example inputs with what a good output looks like.
  2. Run them through two or three candidate models with the same prompt.
  3. Score quality, speed and cost per request side by side.
  4. Pick the cheapest model that meets your quality bar, and keep the test set for every future change.

We also build the app so the model can be swapped behind one interface. Prices and quality change quickly, and being locked to one provider is a risk you do not need.

Seven pitfalls in generative AI projects

1. Building on a demo, not a test set

A few impressive examples do not prove the app works. Without a fixed set of test inputs, nobody can tell whether a prompt change made things better or worse. Build the test set before the product.

2. Trusting the output too much

Models can state wrong facts confidently. For anything that matters, such as prices, legal text, medical or financial content, ground the model in your own data, show sources, and keep a human review step until error rates are known.

3. Calling the model from the user's device

Putting an API key inside a mobile app or web page means anyone can copy it and run up your bill. Model calls belong on your server, with authentication and per-user limits.

4. No cost ceiling

One user with a script can generate thousands of requests overnight. Set daily limits per user and per account, alerts on spend, and a hard cap with your provider.

5. Ignoring prompt injection

If your app reads emails, web pages or uploaded files, those texts can contain instructions aimed at the model. Treat outside content as data, limit what the model is allowed to do, and require approval for actions that cannot be undone.

6. Unclear rights and privacy

Know where user data goes, how long it is kept and whether a provider may use it. For image apps, check the provider's terms on ownership and commercial use of outputs, and filter prompts that ask for harmful content or real people. If you serve users in the EU, data protection rules and the EU AI Act's transparency duties may apply; take legal advice for your case.

7. Slow responses

Large models can take several seconds to answer, and image generation longer. Stream text as it is written, show progress, run long jobs in the background and notify the user when they finish. Speed is part of quality.

A sensible build plan

  1. Discovery (1 to 2 weeks): define the one job the app must do well, collect example inputs and outputs, estimate running cost.
  2. Proof of concept (2 to 4 weeks): test models and prompts against the examples. Stop here if quality is not good enough, before spending on the full product.
  3. MVP (6 to 12 weeks): accounts, payments, limits, the core generation flow and an admin view. Our guide to what to build first in an MVP helps keep scope tight.
  4. Launch and learn: review real outputs every week, add failures to the test set and tune prompts or models.

Questions clients ask

How much does it cost to build a generative AI app?

An AI feature added to an existing product usually takes 60 to 160 hours, about $3,000 to $8,000 at $50 per hour. A standalone app with accounts, subscriptions and usage limits is often 250 to 500 hours, about $12,500 to $25,000. Model usage and hosting are extra each month.

Should I use OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or an open-source model?

Test two or three on your own examples and pick the cheapest that meets your quality bar. Hosted models are easiest and strongest for complex tasks; open-source models suit strict data rules or very high volume. Build so the model can be swapped later.

How do I stop AI running costs getting out of control?

Keep model calls on your server, set per-user daily limits and spend alerts, cache repeated results, keep prompts short, and send simple requests to smaller models. Price subscriptions to cover your heaviest users, not the average.
