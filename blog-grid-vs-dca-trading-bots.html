Grid vs DCA trading bots: how each works

By Awais Chaudhry, 28 November 2026

Grid bots and DCA bots are two of the most common automated strategies on crypto exchanges, and they are often confused. Both place orders in steps, both are easy to describe and both look calm on a backtest chart. They behave very differently when the market moves hard. This guide explains how each one works, with worked numbers, what each needs to run safely, and what a custom version costs to build. It is written from a developer's point of view: a bot automates a strategy you define, it does not create returns, and trading always carries the risk of loss.

The short answer

  • A grid bot places a ladder of buy orders below the price and sell orders above it, inside a range you choose. Each time the price moves from one line to the next, it buys low and sells one step higher. It is built for prices that move up and down inside a range.
  • A DCA bot (dollar-cost averaging) opens a position with a base order, adds more in safety orders as the price falls, and sells the whole position when the price rises a set percentage above the average entry. It is built for buying dips and exiting on a bounce.
  • The key risks differ. A grid bot's main risk is the price leaving the range. A DCA bot's main risk is running out of safety orders while the price keeps falling, leaving you holding a large position at a loss.

How a grid trading bot works

You set a lower price, an upper price, the number of grid lines and how much to put on each line. The bot splits the range into levels, places limit buy orders on the levels below the current price and limit sell orders on the levels above it. When a buy fills, it places a sell one level higher. When that sell fills, it places a buy one level lower again. Each completed buy-then-sell pair is one grid profit, before fees.

A worked grid example

Say a coin trades at 100 USDT. You set a range of 90 to 110 with lines every 2 USDT. If the price dips to 98, the buy at 98 fills and the bot places a sell at 100. If the price returns to 100, that sell fills: a gross gain of 2 USDT on 98, about 2.04%. If your exchange charges 0.1% per side, round-trip fees take about 0.2%, so the step earns about 1.84% before slippage on the amount placed on that line. The bot repeats this every time the price crosses lines in either direction.

The same arithmetic shows the trap: if the lines were only 0.2% apart, fees would eat the whole step. Grid spacing must be comfortably wider than your round-trip fees. More lines means more, smaller trades; fewer lines means fewer, larger ones.

Arithmetic vs geometric grids

An arithmetic grid spaces lines by a fixed price amount (every 2 USDT). A geometric grid spaces them by a fixed percentage (every 2%). Geometric grids keep the percentage gain per step the same across a wide range, so they usually suit wider ranges; arithmetic grids are simpler and fine for narrow ones.

What happens when the price leaves the grid

This is the part that decides whether a grid bot is safe. If the price rises above the top line, the bot has sold everything and stops trading; you keep the gains but miss the rest of the rise. If the price falls below the bottom line, every buy has filled and the bot holds the full position while it keeps falling. A well-built bot needs a rule for both cases that you choose in advance: stop and alert, close the position at a stop-loss, wait for the price to return, or move the grid. It should never silently keep buying.

How a DCA trading bot works

A DCA bot has a few settings: the base order size, the safety order size, the price drop that triggers each safety order (deviation), how many safety orders are allowed, optional step and volume scaling, and a take-profit percentage measured from the average entry price. Each safety order lowers the average price, so the exit point moves closer to the current price.

A worked DCA example

A base order of $100 buys at 100. Safety orders trigger every 2%, and each one doubles the previous safety order: $100 at 98, $200 at 96 and $400 at 94. If all three fill, you have spent $800 and the average entry is about 95.70. With a 1.5% take profit, the bot sells everything at about 97.14, well below the 100 where it started. That is the appeal: the price does not need to fully recover.

The same example shows the cost. A "$100 bot" actually needs $800 of capital to run all its safety orders. And if the price keeps falling after the last safety order, there is nothing left to average with: at 90 the position is worth about $752, and at 80 about $669. Scaling each safety order up (volume scaling) is close to a martingale approach, which grows the position fastest exactly when the market is moving against it. A good DCA bot shows you the full capital required and the worst-case drawdown before it starts, and supports a hard stop-loss.

Neither is "better". They automate different ideas, and many clients run both on different pairs or switch based on rules they define. Some bots combine them, for example a DCA entry with a grid exit. Whatever the mix, you decide the strategy; the developer's job is to execute it exactly and handle the edge cases.

Spot or futures?

Both bots can run on spot or futures markets. On spot, the worst case is holding a coin that has fallen in value. On futures with leverage, the worst case is liquidation, where the exchange closes the position and the margin is lost. Leverage also adds funding payments. We suggest building and testing on spot first, and if you do use futures, adding a liquidation-distance check and hard limits on leverage and position size.

What a production grid or DCA bot needs

The strategy logic is the easy part. Most of the build time goes into making the bot behave correctly when real exchanges misbehave:

  • Exchange rules. Every pair has a minimum order size, minimum order value, price tick size and quantity step. Orders that break them are rejected, so the bot must round correctly.
  • State recovery. After a restart or a lost connection, the bot must reload its open orders and fills from the exchange and carry on, never place duplicates.
  • Order reconciliation. Partial fills, cancelled orders and missed WebSocket messages need to be checked against the exchange regularly.
  • Fees and balances. Fees may be charged in the traded coin or a separate token, which changes the amount available to sell.
  • Rate limits. A grid with many lines can hit the exchange's request limits, so orders are placed and refreshed in controlled batches.
  • Safe API keys. Trade-only keys with withdrawals disabled and, where the exchange supports it, an IP allowlist. Keys are stored encrypted, never in code.
  • Risk limits and a kill switch. Maximum capital, stop-loss, maximum open positions, and a single command that cancels all orders.
  • Alerts and a panel. Telegram or Discord messages for fills, errors and range breaks, and a simple dashboard to change settings without editing code.
  • Backtesting with fees. Testing on historical data including fees and realistic fills, then on the exchange's testnet or with small amounts, before any larger capital.

Ready-made bots vs a custom build

Several exchanges offer built-in grid and DCA bots, and hosted platforms and open-source tools such as Freqtrade and Hummingbot cover the standard versions. If the standard settings fit your plan, start there; it costs little. A custom bot makes sense when you need your own entry rules or indicators, several exchanges in one system, custom risk rules, your own dashboard or reporting, or a strategy the ready-made tools cannot express. Our Freqtrade vs Hummingbot vs custom bot comparison covers that choice in more detail. The same ideas also apply to forex: grid and averaging logic can be built as an MT5 Expert Advisor, but many prop firms restrict grid or martingale-style strategies, so check your firm's rules first.

Questions traders ask

Is a grid bot or a DCA bot better?

Neither is better in general. A grid bot is designed for prices that move up and down inside a range, and a DCA bot for buying pullbacks and exiting on a bounce. Both can lose money in a strong trend. Choose based on the strategy you want to automate, then test it with fees on historical data and small amounts first.

How much money do I need to run a DCA bot?

Add up the base order and every safety order. With volume scaling, the total can be many times the base order, for example $800 for a $100 base order with three doubling safety orders. A good bot shows this full figure and the worst-case drawdown before it starts.
