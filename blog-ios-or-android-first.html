iOS or Android first: which platform should your app launch on?

By Awais Chaudhry, 20 November 2026

You have an app idea, a budget and a deadline, and someone asks the question every founder hits early: should we launch on iPhone or Android first? Building for both at once is often possible, but it is not always the right first move. Picking one platform can cut your first release cost, shorten the time to real user feedback and keep your team focused. This guide explains how to make that decision from your own users, money and product, not from habit or personal phone preference.

The short answer

  • Launch on iOS first if your first customers are mostly in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, Western Europe or Japan, if you plan to charge for subscriptions or in-app purchases, or if your target users are professionals and consumers who tend to carry iPhones in your market.
  • Launch on Android first if your users are in South Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Middle East or much of Southeast Asia, if the app runs on company-issued or rugged devices, or if it must work on low-cost phones for field staff.
  • Launch on both at once if your users are split across both platforms and you cannot afford to turn half of them away, which is common for marketplaces, booking apps and B2B tools used by a whole workforce. A cross-platform framework such as Flutter or React Native usually makes this affordable.

The rest of this article explains each factor so you can check which answer fits your case.

Start with where your users are

This is the single most important factor, and it is the one most often guessed. Worldwide, Android runs on far more phones than iOS. But worldwide numbers do not matter; your market does. In the United States, the UK, Australia and Japan, iPhones are a very large share of the phones in use, and among some groups, such as students or high earners, they can be the clear majority. In Pakistan, India, Indonesia, Brazil, Nigeria and many other countries, Android is dominant by a wide margin.

Before you decide, get evidence about your actual audience:

  • Website analytics. If you already have a website, Google Analytics or a similar tool shows the share of mobile visits from iOS and Android. This is the best free signal you have.
  • Customer interviews. Ask ten target customers what phone they use. It takes an afternoon and removes a lot of guesswork.
  • Who pays. For B2B apps, find out whether staff use their own phones or company devices. Many companies issue Android devices to field teams and iPhones to managers, or the reverse.
  • Public market data. Sites such as StatCounter publish mobile OS share by country. Use the latest figures for the countries you are targeting.

Revenue: which platform earns more from apps?

App store reports have consistently shown that iOS users spend more on apps and in-app purchases than Android users, even though there are fewer of them. If your business model depends on people paying inside the app, such as subscriptions, premium features or paid content, iOS first is often the safer bet in Western markets.

The picture changes for other business models:

  • Ad-supported apps need volume, which can favour Android in large Android-heavy markets.
  • Apps that sell physical goods or services (food delivery, bookings, marketplaces) take payment through your own payment provider, so store spending habits matter less than where your customers are.
  • Internal business tools earn nothing in the store at all. The deciding factor is which devices your staff have.

Also check the store rules for your model. Both Apple and Google require their own in-app purchase systems for many digital goods, with commission, while physical goods and real-world services can use providers such as Stripe. Getting this wrong is a common cause of rejection, so decide your payment flow before you build.

Development cost and time: iOS vs Android

Building natively

If you build natively, with Swift for iOS and Kotlin for Android, each platform is its own codebase, and launching on one platform first roughly halves the app part of the first build. The backend, admin panel, design and API work are shared and do not double. For native builds, iOS has historically been a little quicker to develop and test because there are far fewer device models and screen sizes to support. Android needs testing across more manufacturers, screen sizes and OS versions, and some manufacturers change how background tasks and notifications behave.

Building cross-platform

With Flutter or React Native, most of the code is written once and runs on both. Adding the second platform then costs a fraction of the first, mainly for platform-specific testing, store setup and any native features. That changes the question from "which platform first?" to "do we release both on day one, or release one first and keep the other ready for a few weeks later?" Many teams build cross-platform but launch on one store first, so they can fix problems with a smaller group of users before opening up to everyone. App store review and launch speed

Both stores review apps before they go live, but the experience differs:

  • Apple App Store: requires a paid Apple Developer Program membership ($99 per year) and a Mac to build and submit. Apple reviews every release by hand and is strict about privacy details, login options, in-app purchase rules and incomplete features. Most reviews finish within a day or two, but a rejection can add days while you fix and resubmit.
  • Google Play: a one-time $25 registration fee. New personal developer accounts must run a closed test with a group of testers for a set period before they can publish to everyone, so plan for that. Organisation accounts avoid this step but need business verification. Reviews are usually fast, and updates often go live quickly.

Neither store is a reason on its own to pick a platform, but build the review time into your launch plan. Testing early with TestFlight on iOS and internal or closed testing tracks on Google Play catches most problems before the public release.

Features and devices your app depends on

Some apps are pulled towards one platform by what they need to do:

  • Hardware and Bluetooth devices. If your app talks to a specific sensor, scanner or wearable, check which platforms the vendor's SDK supports. Some only support one, or support one much better.
  • Background work and automation. Android allows more control over background tasks, home screen widgets and default apps, which suits utilities and field tools. iOS is more restrictive but more predictable.
  • Apple ecosystem features. Apple Watch apps, HealthKit, Apple Pay and tight integration with Mac and iPad point to iOS first.
  • Kiosks and dedicated devices. Apps that run on a tablet locked to one purpose, or on rugged handhelds, are usually Android.
  • Camera and computer vision. Both platforms are capable. For apps such as sports analysis, the camera quality across devices matters more on Android, where hardware varies widely, so test on the cheaper phones your users actually own.

Testing and quality across devices

An iOS app usually needs testing on a handful of iPhone sizes and the last few iOS versions. An Android app needs a wider spread: different manufacturers, screen sizes, memory levels and OS versions. That does not make Android worse, but it does mean an Android-first launch should include a real device test plan, ideally covering the specific phones common in your market. A simple way to decide

  1. Where are your first 1,000 users? If you can show a clear majority on one platform, start there.
  2. How does the app make money? In-app subscriptions in Western markets lean iOS; volume and ads in Android-heavy markets lean Android; services and B2B follow the users.
  3. Does a must-have feature or device only work well on one platform? If so, that decides it.
  4. Can you afford to turn away half your users? If not, build cross-platform and launch on both, or launch both within a few weeks of each other.
  5. Who will keep the app updated? Two native codebases mean two sets of updates every year. Cross-platform keeps maintenance in one place.

Common mistakes when choosing a launch platform

  • Choosing the platform the founder uses. Your phone is not your market.
  • Building native for one platform with no plan for the other. If you might need both within a year, choose a stack that makes the second platform cheap.
  • Ignoring the web. For some products, a fast web app or a website plus one mobile app reaches more people at lower cost than two native apps.
  • Skipping store preparation. Privacy policies, screenshots, data safety forms and test accounts for reviewers take time. Prepare them alongside development, not after.

Questions founders ask

Is it cheaper to build an iOS or an Android app?

The cost is similar for the same features. Native iOS apps can be slightly quicker to test because there are fewer device models, while Android needs testing on more phones. With Flutter or React Native, adding the second platform costs a fraction of the first.

Should a startup launch on iOS or Android first?

Launch where most of your first users are. iOS first suits subscription apps aimed at the US, UK, Australia and Western Europe. Android first suits markets such as South Asia, Africa and Latin America, and apps for field staff on company devices.

Can I launch on one platform and add the other later?

Yes. If you build with Flutter or React Native, the second platform reuses most of the code and mainly needs testing and store setup. If you build natively, the second app is a separate build, though the backend and designs are reused.
