How much does a trading bot cost to build in 2026?

By Awais Chaudhry, 26 November 2026

"How much does a trading bot cost?" has no single answer, because a bot that places one order when a TradingView alert fires and a system that watches five exchanges for price gaps are very different projects. What you can do is break the bot into the parts that take engineering time, put hours on each, and add the running costs. This guide does exactly that, using our rate of $50 per hour, so you can budget before you talk to any developer.

One point before the numbers: a trading bot automates a strategy that you define. It does not create an edge on its own, nobody can honestly promise it will make money, and trading carries a real risk of loss. Every bot also has to follow the terms of the exchange, broker or prop firm it trades on, and the laws where you live. Good developers build for those limits from the start.

Trading bot cost at a glance

These are typical ranges for a single owner running their own strategy. Your exact number depends on the details covered below.

  • Alert or signal bot (TradingView webhook or simple rules, one exchange or broker): 30 to 60 hours, about $1,500 to $3,000.
  • MT5 Expert Advisor (your rules in MQL5, with risk limits and strategy tester runs): 30 to 80 hours, about $1,500 to $4,000.
  • Grid or DCA crypto bot with a settings panel, Telegram alerts and position tracking: 60 to 120 hours, about $3,000 to $6,000.
  • Multi-strategy bot with backtesting and a dashboard: 120 to 250 hours, about $6,000 to $12,500.
  • Cross-exchange arbitrage or market-making system: 150 to 300 hours or more, about $7,500 to $15,000+.
  • Multi-user platform (accounts, subscriptions, copy trading for many users): usually 400 hours or more, so $20,000+, plus extra legal and compliance work.

What you are paying for: the parts of a trading bot

Almost every bot is built from the same blocks. Counting which ones you need is the fastest way to understand a quote.

1. Market data

The bot needs prices, candles and sometimes the order book. Reading candles from one exchange through its REST API is quick. Streaming order books from several exchanges over WebSockets, reconnecting cleanly when a stream drops and keeping the data in sync takes far longer. Expect 5 to 15 hours for simple data and 30 hours or more for multi-exchange streaming.

2. Strategy logic

This is your rules turned into code: entries, exits, position sizing and filters. If the rules are written clearly ("buy when the 20-period average crosses above the 50, risk 1% per trade, exit at a 2% stop"), this part is often smaller than people expect. Vague rules are what make it expensive, because each "it depends" becomes a question and a revision.

3. Order execution

Placing an order is easy. Placing it correctly every time is not: partial fills, rejected orders, minimum order sizes, price precision, rate limits and network timeouts all need handling. The bot must also remember open positions after a restart. This is where cheap bots usually break, and it is worth 10 to 40 hours depending on the exchange and order types.

4. Risk controls

Maximum position size, daily loss limits, a maximum number of open trades and a kill switch that closes everything and stops trading. For MT5 bots used on prop firm accounts, this includes guards for the firm's daily loss and maximum drawdown rules. Risk controls are not optional extras; we build them into every bot.

5. Backtesting and paper trading

Testing on historical data shows how the rules would have behaved, and paper trading shows whether the live version matches. A careful backtest avoids "look-ahead" mistakes, where the test accidentally uses data the bot could not have known at the time, and includes fees and slippage. Basic backtesting adds 15 to 30 hours; a full research setup with reports and parameter testing can add 60 hours or more. MT5 has its own strategy tester, which keeps this part cheaper for Expert Advisors.

6. Control panel and alerts

You will want to see what the bot is doing. A Telegram bot that reports trades and errors and accepts "pause" and "status" commands is a low-cost option. A web dashboard with charts, settings and trade history costs more but is easier to live with. See our Telegram and Discord bot development page for how alert bots work.

7. Hosting, monitoring and security

The bot runs on a server, restarts automatically, logs everything and alerts you when it fails. API keys are stored securely, trade-only, with withdrawal permission turned off and IP restrictions where the exchange allows them. Setting this up properly is usually 8 to 20 hours.

What pushes the price up

  • More exchanges or brokers. Each one has its own API quirks, symbols, fees and limits. Libraries like ccxt help, but every venue still needs testing.
  • Speed requirements. If the strategy depends on reacting in milliseconds, as arbitrage often does, you need streaming data, efficient code and servers close to the exchange.
  • Complex order types. Trailing stops, OCO orders, futures with leverage and funding payments all add logic and testing.
  • Multiple users. As soon as other people connect their accounts, you need user management, encryption, billing and much stricter security, and you may need legal advice on whether your service is regulated where you and your users live.
  • Unclear strategy. If the rules are still changing, budget for discovery time or start with a small paid proof of concept.

Costs by bot type

Crypto grid and DCA bots

A grid bot places buy and sell orders at set price levels; a DCA bot buys in steps as the price moves and sells at a target. Both are well understood, which keeps cost moderate, but they need careful handling of balances, fees and what happens when the price leaves the grid. Most land in the 60 to 120 hour range with a settings panel and alerts.

MT5 Expert Advisors

An Expert Advisor runs inside MetaTrader 5 and is written in MQL5. A single strategy with standard risk settings is often one of the cheapest bots to build, because the platform already handles data, orders and testing. Costs rise with multiple symbols, news filters, custom indicators and prop firm rule guards. Running it needs a Windows VPS so it stays online.

Arbitrage bots

Arbitrage bots look for price differences between exchanges or trading pairs. The coding is harder than it looks: by the time fees, withdrawal times, slippage and latency are counted, many apparent gaps disappear. A serious build needs live order book data from every venue, fast execution and balance management across exchanges, which is why these sit at the top of the range.

TradingView alert bots

If your strategy already runs in TradingView as a Pine Script, a webhook bot that turns each alert into an order is one of the quickest ways to automate it. The work is mostly in reliable execution and security, so the alert cannot be faked by someone who finds your webhook address.

Running costs after launch

  • Server: a small cloud server or Windows VPS usually costs about $10 to $50 a month for one bot. Low-latency setups cost more.
  • Data: exchange data is usually free through the API; paid historical data or premium feeds are extra.
  • Trading fees: these come from your account and can matter more than any hosting bill for bots that trade often.
  • Maintenance: exchanges change their APIs, deprecate endpoints and adjust limits. Budget a few hours a month, or a support plan, to keep the bot working. Our maintenance and support service covers this.

How to get an accurate quote

  1. Write the rules down. Entries, exits, sizing, and what the bot should do when something unexpected happens.
  2. Name the venue. Which exchange, broker or prop firm, which markets and which account type.
  3. Say how you want to control it. Telegram, a web dashboard, or MetaTrader settings.
  4. Decide on testing. Do you need a full backtest, or will paper trading be enough?
  5. Check the platform's rules. Make sure automated trading is allowed on your account and that the bot can respect any limits the platform sets.

With that, a developer can quote per milestone instead of guessing. We usually split a bot into data and execution, strategy and risk controls, testing, and deployment, so you see working software at each step.

Questions traders ask

How much does a simple trading bot cost?

A bot that follows one clear rule set on one exchange or broker, such as TradingView alerts that place orders, usually takes 30 to 60 hours, or about $1,500 to $3,000 at $50 per hour. Adding a dashboard, several strategies or more exchanges raises the hours.

What does it cost to run a trading bot each month?

Most single-user bots run on a small cloud server or Windows VPS for MetaTrader at roughly $10 to $50 a month. Paid market data, extra servers for low latency and ongoing maintenance for exchange API changes come on top.

Can you guarantee my trading bot will be profitable?

No. Nobody can honestly promise profits. A bot automates the strategy you define, and trading carries real risk of loss. We build and test the strategy as specified, run it on historical data and paper trading first, and add risk limits, but results depend on the strategy and the market.
