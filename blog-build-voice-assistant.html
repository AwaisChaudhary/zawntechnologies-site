How to build a voice assistant for your business

By Awais Chaudhry, 24 November 2026

A voice assistant lets customers or staff get things done by talking: asking about an order, booking an appointment, logging a job from a van, or getting help at a kiosk without touching a screen. In 2026 the building blocks are good enough that a business can launch one without a research team, but the gap between a demo and something people actually like using is still wide. This guide explains how a business voice assistant works, the decisions you need to make, and how to build one step by step.

What a business voice assistant can do

Start with the job, not the technology. The voice assistants that work best handle a narrow set of frequent, well-defined tasks:

  • Answering the phone: opening hours, prices, order status, and routing callers to the right person.
  • Bookings and appointments: checking a calendar, offering slots, confirming by text message.
  • Lead capture: asking a few qualifying questions out of hours and sending a summary to your CRM.
  • Hands-free work: field staff dictating job notes, checklists or stock counts while their hands are busy.
  • Kiosks and avatars: a talking assistant on a screen in a shop, office or exhibition stand.
  • Internal help: staff asking spoken questions about procedures and getting answers from company documents.

If you cannot write down the five or ten things callers or users ask most often, do that first. It becomes your scope, your test script and your success measure.

How a voice assistant works

Most business voice assistants are a pipeline of four parts, with a fifth that connects them to your systems.

1. Speech recognition (speech to text)

The assistant listens and turns speech into text. Popular options include OpenAI's Whisper models, Deepgram, Google Cloud Speech-to-Text and Azure Speech. For live conversation you need streaming recognition, which returns words as the person speaks, rather than waiting for a full recording. Accuracy depends heavily on accents, background noise, phone audio quality and specialist vocabulary such as product names, so test with real recordings from your users.

2. Understanding and deciding (the language model)

The transcribed text goes to a large language model with instructions about who it is, what it may do and how to behave. The model works out what the person wants and writes a reply. For questions about your business, it should answer from your own content using retrieval, which we explain in how RAG works, rather than from its general knowledge.

3. Taking actions (tools and integrations)

A useful assistant does more than talk. Through function calling, the model can check a booking calendar, look up an order, create a ticket or send a text message. Each action is a small, tested piece of code with clear rules, for example "only offer slots in the next 14 days" or "never cancel an order without reading back the order number". This is where most of the real engineering, and most of the business value, sits.

4. Speaking back (text to speech)

The reply is turned into natural speech by a text-to-speech service such as ElevenLabs, Azure Speech, Google or OpenAI. You choose a voice, a speaking speed and how numbers, dates and addresses are read out. Custom or cloned voices are possible, but only with the speaker's clear consent and within the provider's terms.

5. The channel: phone, app, web or device

Phone assistants connect through a telephony provider such as Twilio, Vonage or Telnyx, which streams the call audio to your pipeline. Web and app assistants usually use WebRTC for low-delay audio in the browser or phone. Kiosks and devices need a decent microphone, echo cancellation and sometimes a wake word.

Pipeline or speech-to-speech?

Instead of separate recognition, model and voice steps, newer speech-to-speech models such as the OpenAI Realtime API take audio in and produce audio out directly. They can feel more natural and respond faster, and they handle interruptions well. The separate pipeline gives you more control: you can choose the best recognition for your accents, the best voice for your brand, log clean transcripts and swap any part later. We pick per project; many assistants start on one approach and keep the option to switch.

The hard part: making it feel natural

People are far less patient with a voice assistant than with a chatbot. A pause that is fine in text feels broken on a call. These are the details that separate a good assistant from a frustrating one:

  • Delay. Every stage adds waiting time. Streaming each stage, starting to speak the first sentence while the rest is still being written, and keeping servers close to your users all reduce it. If an action takes time, the assistant should say so ("let me check that for you").
  • Knowing when the person has finished. Turn detection decides when to reply. Too eager and the assistant talks over people; too slow and it feels sluggish. It needs tuning on real conversations.
  • Interruptions. Callers often cut in. The assistant must stop speaking at once and listen, which is called barge-in.
  • Spelling and numbers. Names, email addresses, postcodes and order numbers are where speech recognition fails most. Confirm them back, offer to send a text link, or ask for digits on the keypad.
  • Short answers. Written answers are too long when spoken. Instruct the model to answer in one or two sentences and offer more detail if wanted.
  • A way out. Always let people reach a human, leave a message or get a callback. An assistant that traps callers damages trust quickly.

How to build a voice assistant step by step

  1. Define the tasks and the boundary. List what the assistant must handle, what it must hand over to a person, and what it must never do. Collect real call recordings or typical questions if you have them.
  2. Write the conversation flows. For each task, sketch the happy path and the common problems: unclear answers, missing information, a fully booked day.
  3. Choose the channel and the stack. Phone, web, app or device; pipeline or speech-to-speech; which providers for recognition and voice; where your data is processed.
  4. Build a working prototype quickly. One or two tasks, end to end, on a test number or web page. Listen to it. This shows the delay, voice and accuracy problems far better than any document.
  5. Connect your systems. Calendar, CRM, order database, ticketing, SMS. Add checks so the model cannot take an action without the required details.
  6. Test with real people and real audio. Different accents, noisy rooms, bad mobile lines, people who interrupt, people who go off topic. Keep a test set of conversations and rerun it after every change.
  7. Launch in a limited way. Out of hours only, or one phone line, with call recordings and transcripts reviewed every day at first.
  8. Measure and improve. Track how many conversations finish the task, how many hand over to a person and where people drop off, then fix the top problems each week.

Privacy, consent and compliance

Voice data is personal data. Tell callers they are speaking with an automated assistant and, if you record or transcribe calls, say so at the start. Check where each provider stores and processes audio, whether it uses your data for training, and how long recordings are kept. If you handle health, financial or children's data, or operate under GDPR, plan for data processing agreements, retention limits and access controls from the start. Never let the assistant read out sensitive details without verifying who is calling.

What it costs

There are two kinds of cost: building it, and running it.

Build cost depends on the number of tasks, integrations and channels. As rough guides at our rate of $50 per hour:

  • A web or app voice assistant that answers questions from your documents: often around 80 to 160 hours.
  • A phone assistant that answers calls, books appointments and hands over to staff: often around 200 to 400 hours, depending on integrations and testing.
  • A kiosk or 3D avatar assistant with custom hardware or animation: priced after a short discovery, since the device and visuals drive the effort.

Running cost is mostly usage-based: speech recognition, language model and voice are usually charged per minute or per amount of text, and phone lines add per-minute call charges. Busy phone lines cost more than a web assistant used occasionally. We estimate it from your expected call or conversation volume before building. For a first figure on the build, try our app cost calculator or the AI chatbot cost calculator.

Off-the-shelf platform or custom build?

Hosted voice agent platforms let you set up a basic phone assistant quickly and are a sensible way to test demand. Custom development pays off when the assistant must take real actions in your own systems, follow strict rules, meet privacy requirements, run in your own cloud or on a device, or when per-minute platform fees grow larger than the cost of owning the software. A common path is to prototype on a platform, learn what callers really ask, then build the version you own.

Questions businesses ask

Can a voice assistant answer my business phone calls?

Yes. A telephony provider such as Twilio streams the call to the assistant, which listens, answers from your own information, can book appointments or look up orders, and hands the caller to a person or takes a message when needed.

How long does it take to build a voice assistant?

A working prototype with one or two tasks can often be ready in two to four weeks. A production phone assistant with integrations, testing on real calls and a limited launch usually takes two to three months, depending on scope.

Does a voice assistant understand different accents and languages?

Modern speech recognition handles many languages and accents well, but accuracy varies with audio quality, background noise and specialist vocabulary. We test with recordings from your real users before choosing a provider.
