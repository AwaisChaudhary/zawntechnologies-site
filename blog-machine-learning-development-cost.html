How much does machine learning development cost?

By Awais Chaudhry, 21 November 2026

Ask ten companies what machine learning development costs and you will hear numbers from a few thousand dollars to several hundred thousand. Both can be right. The price of a machine learning project depends far less on the algorithm than on the state of your data, how accurate the model has to be, and where it has to run. This guide breaks the cost down the way we estimate ML projects at ZAWN Technologies, using our own rate of $50 per hour, so you can build a realistic budget before you talk to any vendor.

Machine learning development cost: the short answer

Most machine learning work we are asked about falls into one of three sizes. These are planning ranges for a first version, not fixed quotes, and they assume your data already exists in some usable form.

What drives the cost of a machine learning project

1. The state of your data

This is the biggest factor, and the one most often underestimated. A model learns only from examples. If you have years of clean, consistent records in one database, the data work is modest. If the records are spread across spreadsheets, exports from old systems and free-text notes, someone has to join, clean and check them before any model can learn. On many projects this is 30 to 50 percent of the total effort.

2. Whether the data needs labelling

Supervised learning needs examples with the right answer attached: this invoice is fraudulent, this image shows a damaged part, this customer cancelled. If those labels already exist in your systems, you are in luck. If they do not, they have to be created, often by people who know your business. Image and video projects are the most labour intensive, because every object may need a box or outline drawn around it. Labelling can be done by your staff, by us with your guidance, or by a labelling service, and the choice changes the budget a great deal.

3. How accurate the model needs to be

Getting a model from "useful" to "very good" usually costs more than getting it from nothing to useful. A model that ranks sales leads can be valuable at modest accuracy, because a person still makes the call. A model that rejects transactions or flags safety issues needs a much higher bar, more testing and careful handling of edge cases. Agree the target, and how it will be measured, before development starts.

4. The type of problem

  • Tabular prediction (forecasting, scoring, classification from rows and columns) is usually the cheapest. Well-understood methods such as gradient-boosted trees often perform strongly and train quickly.
  • Text (classifying emails, extracting fields from documents) is mid-range, and today often starts from a pre-trained language model rather than training from scratch.
  • Images and video (detection, tracking, measurement) cost more because of labelling, heavier training and testing across lighting, camera angles and devices. Our article on how computer vision ball tracking works shows how many layers sit behind one feature.
  • Time-critical or streaming systems add engineering for speed and reliability on top of the model itself.

5. Where the model has to run

A model that runs on a cloud server behind an API is the simplest to deploy and update. A model that runs inside a mobile app, a browser or a Raspberry Pi has to be made smaller and faster, converted to formats such as TensorFlow Lite, Core ML or ONNX, and tested on real hardware. That work is worth it when you need offline use, privacy or instant results, but it adds hours.

6. Integration with your systems

A prediction is only useful if it reaches the person or system that acts on it. Connecting the model to your CRM, ERP, website or app, plus a dashboard where staff can review results, is ordinary software development and is often a quarter of the project. Our API development and integration and dashboard services cover this side.

Where the hours go in a typical ML project

  1. Discovery and data audit. We look at the data you have, define what the model must predict, and agree how success is measured. This is where a project can be stopped cheaply if the data cannot support the goal.
  2. Data preparation and labelling. Cleaning, joining, removing duplicates, handling missing values and building labelled training and test sets.
  3. Baseline and experiments. A simple baseline first, then better models. Many projects find that a well-tuned simple model beats a complex one on their data.
  4. Evaluation. Testing on data the model has never seen, checking errors by group and edge case, and comparing against the agreed target.
  5. Deployment. Packaging the model behind an API or into the app, with versioning so a bad model can be rolled back.
  6. Monitoring and retraining. Tracking accuracy and input data over time, and a repeatable pipeline to retrain when the world changes.

Most of this is Python engineering with tools such as pandas, scikit-learn, PyTorch and MLflow, plus cloud and DevOps work for hosting and pipelines.

Running costs after launch

The build is not the only cost. Plan for these ongoing items when you budget:

  • Hosting for predictions. A small model serving a few thousand predictions a day can run on a modest server. Real-time video models or high traffic may need GPU instances, which cost far more per hour.
  • Training compute. Tabular models often train on an ordinary machine. Deep learning on images or video usually needs rented GPUs for hours or days per training run.
  • Retraining. Customer behaviour, prices and products change, and model accuracy slowly drops as they do. This is called model drift. Most production models need retraining on a schedule, from monthly to yearly.
  • Maintenance. Library updates, cloud changes and new data fields need attention. Our maintenance and support plans cover models as well as apps.

Check current pricing with your cloud provider for the instance types you need, because it changes and differs by region.

Do you need custom machine learning at all?

Sometimes the cheapest ML project is the one you do not build. Before you commit to training your own model, check these options:

  • Ready-made APIs. Speech-to-text, translation, general image labelling and optical character recognition are available as paid services that may be good enough.
  • Large language models. Many text tasks, such as summarising, classifying support tickets or extracting fields from documents, can now be done by prompting an existing model, often with a few examples. See our AI and LLM integration service, and our guide to AI chatbot costs if the project is conversational.
  • Rules and reports. If a clear business rule explains most cases, a rule plus a good dashboard can deliver value now while you collect data for a model later.

Custom models earn their cost when the problem is specific to your business, when you have data nobody else has, when per-call API fees would be too high at your volume, or when the data must stay on your own servers or devices.

How to keep machine learning costs under control

  • Start with a paid proof of concept. A few weeks of work on a sample of your real data tells you whether the full project is worth funding.
  • Agree a measurable target. "90 percent of damaged parts flagged, with fewer than one false alarm in twenty" is something an engineer can estimate. "Make it smart" is not.
  • Use pre-trained models. Fine-tuning an existing vision or language model needs far less data and compute than training from zero.
  • Label the right data. Label a smaller, well-chosen set first, then let the model show which examples it struggles with and label those next.
  • Ship a first version to real users. An MVP with a human checking the model's output builds trust and collects the feedback you need to improve it.

Questions about machine learning costs

How much does a machine learning proof of concept cost?

At $50 per hour, a proof of concept usually takes 60 to 160 hours, or about $3,000 to $8,000. It tests whether your data can support the prediction you need and ends with a working prototype and a clear recommendation on whether to build the full system.

Why is data preparation so expensive in ML projects?

A model can only learn from clean, consistent, correctly labelled examples. Joining records from different systems, fixing errors and creating labels is slow, careful work, and on many projects it takes 30 to 50 percent of the total effort.

What are the ongoing costs of a machine learning model?

Plan for hosting to serve predictions, compute for retraining, monitoring for accuracy drops, and routine maintenance of libraries and pipelines. Small tabular models are cheap to run; real-time image or video models on GPUs cost much more.
